# Phase 3.2 - Pre-softmax computational analysis

## Objective
This phase investigates whether attention sink behavior is already present in the pre-softmax attention computation or primarily emerges through the softmax normalisation. R
Rather than analysing only BOS attention probabilities. we decompose the attention computation into its constituent quantities, including BOS logits, competitors logits, logit margins, and measures of competitor structure, to identify which quantities best characterise sink beahvior.

## Relationship between logits and sink probability
### Observation
The BOS logit alone exhibits a weak and highly scattered relationship with BOS attention probability. Heads with similar BOS logits often produce substantially different sink probabilities, indicating that BOS attraction alone is insufficient to determine sink strength.

In contrast, both the strongest-competitor margin and log-sum-exp(LSE) margin show an almost deterministic relationship with BOS attention probability. The empirical observation close follow the theoretical logistic relationship.

$$
p_{BOS} =
\frac{e^m}{1+e^m}
$$

where m denotes the corresponing logit margin.  
Attention entropy decrease monotonically as sink probability increases, showing that stronger sink behavior is associated with increasingl concentrated attention distributions.

### Interpretation
These observation indicate that sink behavior is primarily determined by the relative geometry of the attention logits rather tha n the absolute BOS logit alone. The softmax does not introduce the sink, it rather trasnformed pre-existing logit differences into attention probabilities.

### Conclusion
The logit margin provides a substantially more informative mechanistic quantity than BOS attention probability on BOS logits alone. Consequently, subsequent analyses focus primarily on pre-softmax quantities.

## Structure of competito logits
### Observation
The effective number of competitors,

$$
N_{eff} =
e^H
$$

where $H = p_i \sum(log p_i)$, decreases consistently with layer depth.

Early layers distribute attention across approximately 30 effective competitors, whereas later layers in the model concentrate attention on approximately 4-5 effective competitors.

Although the strongest competitor generally increases with depth, the gap between the first, second, and thrid strongest competitors remains relatively small across many layers.

The LSE margin becomes increasingly correlated with the strongest competitor as depth increases, while the effective number of competitors exhibits a predominantly negative correlation with the BOS margin.

### Interpretation
Attention competition becomes progressively more concentrates throughout the network. However, this concentration does not immediately roduce a single overwhelmingly dominant competitor. Instead, multiple competitors remain relevatn even in later alyers, although th eoverall competitor distribution becomes increasingly sparse.

The competitor LSE therefore capture aggregate competitive pressure but does not uniquely characterize how that competition is distributed among individual tokens.

### Conclusion
The competitive landscape evolves systematically with depth. The reduction in effective competitors indicates increasingly selective attention, while the decomposition of the LSE demonstrates that identical aggregate competition can arise from substantially different competitor structures.

## Shared KV statistical structure
### Observation
Within most layers, the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for BOS logits, competitor LSE, margins, and entropy remains high (approximately 0.7–0.9), indicating strong similarity among heads sharing the same KV group.

However, several layers exhibit substantial reductions in ICC, occasionally approaching zero or negative values.

Within-group and between-group variances likewise vary considerably across layers, with several intermediate layers displaying pronounced variance before gradually decreasing in later layers.

### Interpretation
Heads sharing identical K and V projections frequently exhibit similar computational behavior, but this similaroty is not universal across all layers.

The observed layer-dependent reductions in ICC suggest that substantial head specialisation can arise despite shared memory representations.

Importantly, this anaysis is observation and does not identify the source of that specialisation.

### Conclusion
Grouped Query Attention induces partial—but not complete—coupling among heads sharing the same Key and Value representations. Shared memory representations alone are therefore insufficient to explain all observed sink variability.

## Decomposition of Competitor Evidence
### Observation
The competitor LSE can be decompose into

$$
L = l_{max} + log \sum{e^(l_i - l_{max})}
$$

Across layers, the strongest competitor increasingly dominates the competitor LSE.

Correspondingly, the residual competition term decrease with depth, closely tracking the reduction in the effective number of competitors.

The dominance score increases steadily throughout the network, indicating progressively stronger concentration around the highest-scoring competitor.

### Interpretation
Increasing competitor pressure is not solely explained by growth in the strongest competitor. Early and intermediate layers receive substantial contributions from many moderately competitive tokens, whereas later layers increasingly concentrate competitive evidence around only a few dominant competitors.

### Conclusion
The competitor LSE contains multiple mechanistically distinct components. Decomposing the LSE reveals changes in the internal geometry of competition that are not observable from aggregate competitor statistics alone.

## Variance decomposition of the sink margin
### Observation

The empirical variance of the LSE margin closely matches the theoretical identity

$$
Var(m)=Var(B)+Var(L)−2Cov(B,L)
$$

confirming the correctness of the computational decomposition.

Across most layers, BOS logits and competitor LSE exhibit very strong positive covariance, with correlations approaching one.

Consequently, increases in BOS logits are generally accompanied by corresponding increases in aggregate competitor evidence.

The covariance term contributes substantially to the observed margin variance throughout much of the network.

## Interpretation
Sink margins are not generated by independent variation in BOS attraction and competitor suppression. Instead, BOS and competitor logits evolve together, indicating that they respond to shared changes in the underlying attention computation.

Because analysis pools observations across heads, query positions, and sequence within each layer, it characterises overall computational variability rather than isolating head-specific specialisation.

## Conclusion
The sink margin is best understood as a property of the relative geometry between BOS and competitor logits rather than either quantity in isolation. The strong coupling between these quantities motivates the subsequent investigation of head specialization within shared KV groups.

## Overall conclusionn
This phase demonstrates that attention sink behavior is fundamentally a pre-softmax phenomenon. While BOS attention probabilities provide a useful descriptive measure of sink strength, they obscure the computational mechanisms that generate those probabilities.

- Relative logit geometry dominates sink behavior. Logit margins explain BOS attention probabilities far more effectively than BOS logits alone, indicating that sink behavior depends on the balance between BOS attraction and competitor evidence
- Attention competition becomes increasingly selective with depth. The effective number of competitors decreases substantially across layers, while the strongest competitors become progressively more influential, revealing a systematic evolution in the structure of competition.
- Shared KV representations do not fully determine sink behavior. Although heads within the same KV group frequently exhibit similar behavior, layer-dependent reductions in group similarity demonstrate that shared memory representations alone cannot explain all sink variability.
